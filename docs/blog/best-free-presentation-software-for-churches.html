Best free presentation software for churches: what to look for

A fair look at free church presentation software: the categories, what free usually costs you, and a checklist to test any option before Sunday.

The church budget has no line for software. Maybe it never did, or maybe the last paid subscription lapsed and nobody wants to explain the renewal. Either way, you have been asked to find something free that will put lyrics and Scripture on the screen, and you do not want to be the person who picked the tool that fell over in the middle of the second hymn.

"Free" covers a wide range of things, and some of them are very good. This post goes through what free tools tend to look like, what they commonly cost you in other ways, and how to test one properly before you trust it with a service.

What free usually means

Not all free software is free in the same way. Before comparing features, work out which kind you are looking at.

  • Free and open source. The code is public, anyone can inspect and improve it, and it is typically maintained by volunteers. OpenLP and FreeShow are in this group. The cost is usually your own time: reading documentation, asking in a forum, and accepting that fixes arrive when a volunteer has time.
  • Free to use, not open source. A person or company gives the app away without publishing the code. You get the software, not the right to modify it. Vifug is in this group.
  • Free tier of a paid product. The basic version costs nothing, and features you will want (more screens, streaming, a bigger library) sit behind a subscription. This can be fine as a trial, but check what the free version removes. Some add a watermark or limit what you can save.
  • Free trial. It works fully for a while, then stops. This is a test drive, not a free option, so do not plan a church around it.
  • General slide software. PowerPoint, Keynote and Google Slides may already be available to you. They are not dedicated church tools, but they are a legitimate free starting point. See when they are enough.

Pricing changes often, so look at each product's own pricing page rather than trusting a list, including this one.

What a church actually needs from the software

Strip away the feature lists and a service has a small number of recurring jobs. Whatever you pick should do these without drama.

  1. Show song lyrics in a readable size, split into sensible slides, quickly searchable by title.
  2. Show a Bible verse on request, in the translation your pastor reads from.
  3. Show announcements, pictures and short videos.
  4. Let you check before the congregation sees it. A private preview stage and a separate live output.
  5. Handle a second screen without a tangle of settings.
  6. Keep working when the internet does not.

Beyond these, the next layer is nice to have rather than essential: a stage display for the band, a phone remote, a stream overlay, service plans. Decide which of those your church would genuinely use. A big feature list is only useful if you will touch the features.

The honest costs of free

If the price is zero, something else is paying. Often it is you, in smaller ways.

Your volunteers' time

A tool with a steep learning curve costs hours that someone has to give. Estimate how long it takes a new volunteer to run a song without help. If the answer is a whole afternoon, factor that in.

Support

Paid products usually have a support line. Free ones often have a forum, a guide and a developer who answers email when possible. That can be perfectly adequate, but know what you are getting before the night before a major service.

Content

Some tools come empty. You type or import every song yourself. Others ship with a starting library. That saves real time on the first Sunday. Check whether the songs and Bible versions included are ones you will use, and make sure you are licensed to display any modern song you add. Most current worship songs are copyrighted, so a licence such as CCLI is usually the church's responsibility, whatever software shows the words.

Longevity

Free projects can slow down or end. Open source gives you a safety net, because others can carry it on. For anything else, keep your library exportable. Whatever you choose, make regular backups and know how to move your songs out.

Polish

Some free tools look rougher or have quirks. That matters less than reliability, but test how it feels for a nervous volunteer on a Sunday morning.

How to test any option before Sunday

Do not decide from screenshots. Spend an hour with a real service plan.

  1. Install it on the actual computer that will run Sunday, and connect the actual projector or TV.
  2. Load five real songs from last month. Time how long it takes to find and cue each.
  3. Put up three Bible verses in your preferred translation.
  4. Import one existing deck or one picture and one video.
  5. Pull the network cable. Run through it all again.
  6. Hand it to a newer volunteer with no coaching. Watch where they hesitate.
  7. Look at the screen from the back row. Is the text large enough?
  8. Make a backup and confirm you can restore it.

Whichever tool survives that hour is a candidate. For a more formal comparison, how to choose presentation software has a scoring table.
