Service planning and run sheets for the media desk

How to build a service run sheet with the right order, cues, timers and announcements, and how to turn it into a plan, timer and ticker in Vifug.

At 10:42 the pastor says "let's turn to the passage" and the operator realises nobody told them which passage. Someone whispers a book and chapter. The operator hunts for it with a hundred people watching a blank wall. A run sheet, written by Thursday and shared with everyone who touches the service, prevents nearly every moment like that. It does not have to be long. It has to exist, be accurate and be where the operator can see it.

Here is how to build one, what goes in it, and how to carry it into your presentation software so the Sunday desk is mostly pressing the next button.

What a run sheet is for

A run sheet is the service in order, one line per moment, with a time and an owner. It is different from a bulletin, which tells the congregation what is happening. A run sheet tells the team what to do, in what order, who is responsible, and what the screen should show.

A good one answers five questions for every line:

  • What happens?
  • Who leads it?
  • How long should it take?
  • What is on the screen while it happens?
  • What is the cue that tells the operator to move on?

Build it in this order

  1. Fix the start and end times. Say 9:00 to 10:30. Everything must fit inside that.
  2. Place the fixed points. The sermon, communion or offering, and any item that cannot move.
  3. Add the songs and readings. Songs by title, readings by reference, such as "Psalm 23" or "John 3:16-21".
  4. Add the connective bits. Welcome, prayers, announcements, transitions. These are where most surprises hide.
  5. Put in the screen state. For each line, note whether the wall should show words, a slide, a logo, a blank or a countdown.
  6. Add times. Start with realistic estimates. A four-verse hymn takes longer than people expect.
  7. Send it to everyone. The pastor, the worship leader, the sound operator, the person who runs the slides, and the person who runs the announcements.

Cues: how the operator knows when to move

A cue is a thing the operator can hear or see. "When the music ends" is a cue. "At about 9:25" is not, because services drift. Prefer these:

  • A spoken phrase: "Let us pray", "Please stand".
  • A musical event: the last line of the final chorus, the key change.
  • A person's action: the reader steps to the lectern.

Agree the cues with the people leading, not only among the media team. If the worship leader says "one more time" at the end of a song and the operator does not know that means repeat the chorus, the screen will be wrong. Write down what the leader's habits are and how the screen should respond.

Timers

Timers do two jobs: they keep the service on schedule, and they help the person on the platform. Think about where each timer should be visible.

  • Pre-service countdown. People who arrive early like to know when things begin, and it gives the team a fixed deadline. Five or ten minutes is typical.
  • Speaker timer. The preacher sees how long is left. This belongs on the stage monitor only. The congregation does not need it.
  • Stream countdown. If you stream, a countdown on the online feed lets viewers know when the broadcast begins.

Be honest about timings when you write them down. If you know the sermon runs 35 minutes, put 35, not 25.

Announcements

Announcements are the most common cause of over-running. Put a limit on them and stick to it. Some practical rules:

  • Keep it to three items or fewer. The rest go in the bulletin or an email.
  • One slide per item, with a big headline, a date and a place. Not a paragraph.
  • Decide who writes them, and by when. Midweek is a good deadline.
  • Use a scrolling line for notices that must reach everyone but do not deserve a stop in the service, such as a car that needs moving or a change of room.

Turning the run sheet into a Vifug service plan

Once you have the paper version, put the same thing into the software, so the order is a list on the screen rather than a page on the desk. In Vifug this is the Plans tab, described in the service plans chapter. You create a plan, give it a name such as "Sunday 9am", and optionally set a service date. Then you add the items:

  • Songs from the library.
  • Scripture references.
  • Header labels for sections, for example "Announcements".
  • Blank spacers.

Drag to reorder. On the day, click Cue on any item during the service and Vifug switches to the right tab and loads it, ready for Preview and then Live. That means the operator moves from item to item without searching, but still checks every item in Preview before sending it. You keep the safety of the Preview to Live step and lose the hunting.

One limitation worth knowing: according to the guide, when you move a library to another computer, settings, service plans and live history are not carried across. If you need the same plan on a second machine, rebuild it there or keep a printed copy.

Countdown timer and announcement ticker in Vifug

The Timer panel in the left sidebar runs a countdown or a count-up over the output. Set the minutes and seconds, or use one of the 5m, 10m, 15m or 30m presets, then press Start. You can pause, resume and reset from the same panel. Under Show it on, pick Main screen, Stage, Stream, or any combination. For a pre-service countdown, choose Main screen. For a preacher's clock, choose Stage only. The guide also notes that all screens read the same second even if the machines disagree about the time.

The announcement ticker is a line of text scrolling along the bottom of the screen. You set it in Settings, then General, then Announcement ticker: turn it on, type the message, choose the speed and colours. It keeps scrolling even when the screen is blank, shows on the projector and the stream overlay, and never appears on your own operator preview. Switch it off when the notice has served its purpose, because a ticker nobody needs any more is one people learn to ignore. Both are covered in the timer, announcement and logo chapter.

When the plan changes on the day

It will change. The preacher adds a song, a visitor is introduced, the baptism runs long. A run sheet is a plan you can depart from, not a script you defend. A few habits make changes painless:

  • Have one person, usually the team lead, take change requests. Everyone else sends requests to them, not to the operator at the desk.
  • Repeat any change back out loud: "so after song two we go straight to the reading".
  • Keep a few spare items ready: a blank, a logo, and a generic Scripture slide, so you can fill a gap calmly.
  • Cut from the end, not the middle. If time is short, drop the last item that nobody will miss, and tell the leaders early.
  • Write the change on the paper sheet so the debrief has a record.

Before and after the service

  1. Thursday: first draft of the run sheet goes to the leaders.
  2. Friday: songs, readings and announcements are confirmed. Fix spellings.
  3. Saturday or early Sunday: load the plan, check each item, and run through it once without the congregation.
  4. During the service: follow the cues, and mark any changes on the paper copy.
  5. After: write down what ran long or got skipped, so next week's times are more honest.
