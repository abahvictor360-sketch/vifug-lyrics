Setting up a projector and second screen for church

How to cable a projector, choose mirror or extend, match resolution and aspect ratio, test brightness, and assign the output screen in Vifug.

The service starts in twenty minutes, the laptop is open, and the wall is still showing a blue "No signal" box. Or worse, the projector works but the words are cut off at the top, the picture is stretched, and the operator's controls are sitting on the big screen for everyone to read. Almost every one of these problems comes from the same few decisions about cables, display mode and resolution, and you can settle them once on a quiet weekday instead of fixing them live.

This guide walks through that setup in the order I would do it: cable, display mode, resolution, brightness, a proper test, and then telling your presentation software which screen is the audience's.

Choose and run the cable

Most laptops and projectors today meet at HDMI. Older projectors still use VGA, and many thin laptops only have USB-C or DisplayPort, which need an adapter. Whatever you have, a few practical points save you grief:

  • Keep runs short. A standard HDMI cable is happy up to a few metres. Past roughly 10 metres, a plain cable can give flicker, sparkles or no picture at all. For a long run to a ceiling projector, use an active HDMI cable, an HDMI extender over network cable, or a wireless HDMI kit made for the purpose.
  • Avoid adapter chains. USB-C to HDMI to VGA is three things that can fail. One good adapter is fine; a daisy chain is a Sunday morning gamble.
  • Secure the plug at the laptop end. A cable that falls out when someone brushes the table is the most common live failure. Tape it down or use a cable clip.
  • VGA carries no sound and no handshake. If you are on VGA, the computer may not know the projector's real resolution, so set it by hand (see below).
  • Label both ends and keep a spare cable in the media drawer.

Mirror or extend

Your operating system gives you two sensible modes for a second display.

ModeWhat it doesGood forProblem
Mirror (duplicate)Both screens show the same pictureA quick slideshow where you want to see exactly what the audience seesThe audience sees your desktop, notifications and controls; resolutions are forced to match
ExtendThe projector is a separate second desktopRunning a service with the controls on your screen and only the output on theirsYou need software that knows which screen is which

For a service, extend is almost always the right answer. It keeps your song list, preview and clock off the wall, and lets the projector run at its own best resolution. Mirroring is a fallback when something is behaving strangely and you just need pixels on the wall.

On Windows, press the Windows key and P and pick Extend. On macOS, open System Settings, then Displays, and make sure "Use as" is set to Extended display. On Linux the display settings panel has the same choice, usually under Join Displays or similar.

Resolution and aspect ratio

Every projector has a native resolution, the pixel grid it actually draws. Feeding it something else makes the picture soft or stretched. Common native values are 1280 x 800, 1920 x 1080 and 1920 x 1200. Look at the label on the projector or in its menu, then set the computer's display to the same number in your display settings.

Aspect ratio is the shape of the picture. Modern TVs and many projectors are 16:9. Some older or business projectors are 16:10 or 4:3. If your content is 16:9 and the projector is 4:3, you get black bars top and bottom, which is correct behaviour and better than a squashed picture. If the picture looks stretched, check the projector's own "aspect" or "scaling" menu and set it to match the input, not "fill".

Two other things to check while you are in those menus:

  • Overscan. Some TVs and projectors enlarge the picture slightly and crop the edges. Look for a setting called "Just scan", "Screen fit", "1:1" or "Overscan off" and turn it on so the whole image is visible.
  • Keystone. If the projector sits low or off to the side, the picture becomes a trapezoid. Fix this with the lens shift or by moving the projector if you can, because digital keystone correction softens the image.

Even with all that right, keep your text away from the extreme edges. A margin of around 5 percent of the screen width on each side is a sensible habit, and more on a projector you cannot fully trust. You will thank yourself when the next person changes the zoom.

Brightness and room light

Projector brightness is measured in lumens, and the number you need depends on the room, not only the screen size. A dim hall with the lights off can get away with a modest projector. A bright room with big windows needs much more, and even then it helps to close curtains or switch off the lights nearest the screen.

  • Dark backgrounds with light text read better in a bright room than light backgrounds, because the projector only adds light and cannot make black. A dark navy background with white words is forgiving.
  • Test with the real room lights on, not in the dark during setup.
  • Check the lamp or laser hours if your projector reports them. A dim picture that used to be fine is often just an old lamp.
  • Clean the filter. Overheating projectors dim themselves to protect the lamp.

Test it properly before Sunday

A test is more than "something appeared". Run through this once after any change to the room or hardware, and keep a printed copy by the desk:

  1. Power on the projector first and wait until it shows a stable picture. Then connect or wake the laptop.
  2. Confirm the display mode is Extend and the projector resolution matches its native value.
  3. Put up a slide with text in all four corners. Check nothing is clipped.
  4. Put up a slide with a solid white and a solid black background. Look for dead patches, colour tint or a visible lamp hot spot.
  5. Show a lyric with long lines and a Bible verse with a long reference. Read both from the back row.
  6. Walk the room. Stand where the person furthest away sits and where someone near a window sits.
  7. Unplug and replug the cable while the software is running, so you know what happens when someone trips it.

Assigning the output screen in Vifug

This is the step that confuses people in other tools, so it is worth describing how Vifug handles it. Plug in a projector or TV and Vifug puts the output on it by itself, full screen, without being asked. If you connect the screen in the middle of a service, the same thing happens with no restart. If the machine has only one display, Vifug leaves it alone, because covering your own controls with the output would be worse than showing nothing.

Under the Preview and Live thumbnails there is one line that tells you what is happening: it reads Projecting on and the screen name, or No second screen connected, or Output closed, with a Close or Project link at the end.

If you have more than one screen and need to pick, right-click either the Preview or the Live thumbnail. The menu lists every connected screen by name and resolution. Click one and the output moves there immediately. The screen currently on air is marked, and your own is labelled so you do not choose it by accident. Choosing a screen also remembers it, so next Sunday with the same screens plugged in Vifug opens on the one you used.

A few details that matter in practice:

  • If you close the output deliberately, it stays closed until you project again or plug or unplug a screen. Vifug will not reopen it a moment after you turned it off.
  • Settings, then General, then Projector output has the same controls with more room: a Turn on or Turn off button, every connected screen with a Send here button, and an option to forget the preferred screen.
  • If a second monitor is used for recording rather than for the congregation, switch off Project automatically in the same place.
  • The keyboard shortcut F9 opens or closes the projector, and Esc on the projector screen closes it. You can rebind these in Settings, then Shortcuts.

Vifug also has a text margin setting, so if the projector crops the top edge you can pull the words in a little instead of redesigning every slide. It is described in the Look and feel chapter of the guide.

A short pre-service routine

  • Projector on, cable seated, laptop on mains power.
  • Display mode Extend, projector at native resolution.
  • Output on the right screen, with the status line reading Projecting on and the right name.
  • Corners test slide checked once.
  • A blank, logo or welcome slide up as people arrive, so the wall is never empty.
