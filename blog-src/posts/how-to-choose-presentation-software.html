Most software choices go wrong in the same way. Someone watches a demo, loves a feature nobody will use, and picks the tool with the longest list. Three months later the team is fighting it every week, and the one thing they actually needed, say a reliable second screen, was never tested.

This post is a way to avoid that. It is a checklist of six questions about your own situation, then a scoring table you can fill in for each candidate. It works for a church, a school, a venue or an events team. Bring a pen and a real Sunday (or a real event) to measure against.

Step 1: write down what you do now

Before you open a single product page, describe your event in a paragraph. Include:

  • How many people are in the room, and how big the screen is.
  • What goes on it: songs, Scripture, slides, video, a camera, announcements.
  • Who runs it, and how often that person changes.
  • Whether the order is fixed in advance or changes as it happens.
  • Whether it is streamed or recorded.

This paragraph is your yardstick. Every feature you evaluate later either helps with something in it or it does not.

Step 2: the six criteria

1. Team skill

The most capable software is no use if your Sunday operator cannot run it. Be honest about who will sit at the desk. A teenager who volunteers monthly needs something different from a full-time media director.

Test it: hand the app to the least experienced person who might run it, give them a list of five songs, and do not help. Count the hesitations. A good tool gets them through a song within a few minutes, and the quick-start path should be obvious. In Vifug, for instance, the core rhythm is click a slide, check Preview, press Enter or GO LIVE, which is short enough to teach in a rehearsal.

Also ask how forgiving it is. Can a wrong click put the wrong thing on the wall? That leads straight into the next point of safety, covered in the Preview and Live workflow.

2. Hardware

List what you own. The computer's age and operating system, the projector or TV and its connection (HDMI, adapter, wireless), the number of screens, and any capture card or camera.

  • Does the software run on your operating system? Windows, macOS and Linux are not equally supported by every tool.
  • Does it find a second screen on its own, and does it leave your own display alone?
  • If you stream and project from one machine, is the machine strong enough? Streaming uses real processing power.

Test it on the actual machine, with the actual cable. A tool that behaves on a developer's laptop can misbehave on a ten-year-old church PC.

3. Offline operation

Ask the question plainly: if the internet drops during the service, what stops working? Some tools need a connection to open at all. Others need one only for optional features.

The pattern to look for is that everything required to run the event works with no network, and only extras use it. Vifug's desktop app is built that way: songs, the Bible, presentations and the projector work without internet, while streaming, Deepgram speech recognition, online song search, phone camera pairing and update checks need a connection.

Test it: switch off Wi-Fi and run your five songs and three verses.

4. Streaming and recording

If you do not stream, skip this and move on. If you do, decide how you want the words to reach viewers.

  • As a transparent overlay that your streaming program (OBS, vMix) layers over the camera?
  • Through NDI to another machine?
  • Streamed straight from the presentation app to YouTube, Facebook, Twitch or a custom RTMP address?

Also check whether viewers can see something different from the room. Full-screen words suit a congregation, but online viewers often want a lower third over the camera. Vifug supports both from one place, as described in the streaming chapter, and the guide notes that streaming, recording and NDI need the desktop app rather than the browser version.

5. Import and your existing material

You have songs, decks and media already. Moving them should not take a month.

  • What song formats can it import? Plain text, Word documents, files from other church software?
  • How does it treat PowerPoint? Some tools keep the design, some keep only the words. Test one real deck.
  • Can you export or back up your library, so you are never locked in?

Test it: import ten songs and one deck from your own files and look at them on the screen, not in a thumbnail.

6. Support and longevity

Who do you ask when something breaks at 8:45 on a Sunday? Look for a written guide, short videos, an email address that answers, a community forum. Check when the software was last updated. A project that has been quiet for years may not survive the next operating system update.

Think about cost over time too. A free tool has no price but may take longer to learn. A paid tool costs money every month or year. Look at each product's current pricing rather than assuming.

Step 3: score each candidate

Give every candidate a score from 0 to 5 on each criterion, where 0 means it fails your test and 5 means it handles it with no effort. Then multiply by a weight that reflects how much that criterion matters to you. Use weights from 1 (minor) to 3 (critical).

Here is a short worked example. A small church with one volunteer operator and no streaming might weight team skill 3, hardware 3, offline 3, import 2, support 2 and streaming 1. A church that streams every week would weight streaming 3 and perhaps lower the others. The numbers will not make the decision, but they force you to say out loud what you care about, and they expose when you are leaning toward the shiny option for the wrong reasons.

Step 4: three tie-breakers

If two candidates score close, use these.

  1. The nervous volunteer test. Whichever one a newcomer ran more calmly wins.
  2. The exit test. Make a backup or export. Which one lets you leave most easily?
  3. The bad Sunday test. What happens when the second screen is unplugged mid-song? What happens if the app is closed by accident? Pick the one that recovers best.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing for the biggest event, not the usual one. You will run the weekly service fifty times for each Easter.
  • Testing on the wrong machine. Always test on Sunday's computer.
  • Skipping the offline test. The network always fails on the day you least expect it.
  • Letting one enthusiast decide. The person who picks may not be the person who runs it.
  • Forgetting licences. Software does not clear the right to display a copyrighted song. Check your church licensing separately.
