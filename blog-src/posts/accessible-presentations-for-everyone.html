Walk to the back row of your own room on Sunday and look at the screen. Now imagine you are 70, or you left your glasses at home, or you have red-green colour blindness, or you are watching on a phone on a bus. Slides that looked fine at the desk can fail every one of those people. Accessibility is not a special extra for a small group. Most of the rules that make a presentation readable for someone with a disability also make it better for everybody else.

This post gives practical rules you can apply this week: contrast, size, colour, motion, language and support for people who cannot hear. Each one comes with notes on how Vifug's theme options help, but the rules work in any software.

Contrast comes first

Low contrast is the most common failure. Light grey text on a white background, or white text over a bright photograph, is hard to read for anyone and impossible for some.

  • Use light text on a dark background, or dark text on a light one. Avoid mid-tones against mid-tones.
  • Never place text directly over a busy photograph. Darken the picture or put a solid bar behind the words.
  • Test on the real projector. Rooms with daylight wash out the screen, and a contrast that works on a laptop can vanish on a wall.

In Vifug's theme designer you can dim the background picture or video behind the words by 0 to 90 percent. About 30 to 50 percent suits most photographs. You can also add an outline and a shadow to the text, and put an optional solid bar behind the text band, so words stay readable when the picture changes underneath.

Size and weight

Text that is comfortable at your desk is usually too small at the back of the room. A rough way to check: stand at the furthest seat and read a full slide without effort.

  • Keep slides short. Two to four lines of lyrics, or one to two verses of Scripture, is a good ceiling.
  • Favour a heavy weight. A weight of 600 to 700 reads well from the back of a room, and very thin fonts do not.
  • Choose a clear typeface. Avoid decorative or script fonts for body text, and avoid long runs of capitals, which are harder to read.
  • Leave space around the words. Margins and line spacing are part of readability.

If a slide has too many words, split it, instead of letting the font shrink. The Lines per slide setting re-paginates a song instantly, so you can test two lines against four. Also set the Text margin to keep words clear of an edge your projector crops.

Colour, and people who cannot tell colours apart

Many people have some form of colour blindness, and red and green are the usual trouble. Never use colour as the only signal.

  • Do not use red and green together to mean different things, such as one language in red and another in green.
  • If you use colour to separate two lines, also separate them by position, size or a gap.
  • Prefer pairs with a strong difference in lightness, such as white on dark blue or black on yellow, because lightness difference survives most kinds of colour blindness.
  • Check your design in greyscale. If two elements look the same in grey, they may look the same to some viewers.

When Vifug shows a translation line beside the main text, you can choose its colour. Choose one that differs strongly in lightness from the main line, and keep the line in a different position so colour is not doing all the work.

Motion and flicker

Animation can be pleasant, but it can also be a problem. Fast flashing can trigger seizures in people with photosensitive epilepsy, and constant movement is distracting or nauseating for many others.

  • Never use anything that flashes rapidly. If in doubt, leave it out.
  • Prefer a gentle fade for slide changes, and keep entrances short.
  • Be careful with looping background video. Slow, soft motion is fine. Fast cuts, strobing lights and big camera shakes are not.
  • Keep the text still. Moving text is hard to read for everyone.

In the Vifug theme designer, the Motion group lets you choose how words arrive: Fade in, Rise from below, Drop from above, Slide in, Zoom in or Focus in, with a speed from 0.1 to 2 seconds. None cuts straight in. Half a second is the usual starting point, and a slower speed reads as more gentle. If your room includes anyone sensitive to movement, choose a fade or none, and use a still background. Language and plain wording

Readable does not only mean visible. Words have to be understood.

  • Use plain language on announcements. Short sentences, no jargon, and spell out abbreviations.
  • Offer the language your congregation actually speaks. Vifug includes Bibles in Yoruba, Hausa, Igbo, Twi and Swahili alongside 31 English versions, and lets you add a second-language line to a song.
  • Use a font that supports the characters you need. If a language has accents or special letters, test them on screen before the service.
  • Keep reading order obvious. One idea per slide, top to bottom.

Where possible, ask someone who speaks the language to read the slides before the service. A wrong character or a bad line break can change a meaning.

Hearing-impaired viewers

People who cannot hear, or who hear poorly, rely on the screen for what is said, not only what is sung. Lyrics and Scripture on screen already help. A few more habits go further.

  • Show the words promptly. Lyrics that arrive late are hard to follow, and a late verse breaks the thread of a reading.
  • Put key announcements on screen as well as saying them.
  • For a livestream, keep the words as a lower third over the camera so viewers can see the speaker's face, which helps lip-readers. Vifug can send the online audience a lower third while the room sees full-screen words.
  • Use the screen for the sermon's main points and references, if your team has the time.

Be honest about what software does and does not do. Vifug's AI auto-follow listens to the room to advance slides, but it is not a captioning system, and you should not present it as one. If a live caption service matters for your congregation, plan for it separately.

A quick audit checklist

  1. Read a full slide from the back row. Is it comfortable?
  2. Is there strong contrast, in daylight as well as in the dark?
  3. Does the design still work in greyscale?
  4. Is there anything flashing, spinning or moving fast?
  5. Are there two to four lines at most, and a heavy enough weight?
  6. Is the language plain, and are special characters displayed properly?
  7. Can online viewers see the speaker as well as the words?

Test with real people, not only rules

Guidelines get you most of the way, but nothing replaces asking. Before a service, invite two or three people who do not run the media desk to sit in different parts of the room and read a handful of slides: someone older, someone who wears glasses, someone sitting far to the side. Ask what they could not read, and what they read slowly.

Seat positions matter more than people think. A screen is rarely square to every seat, and glare from windows can hit one side of the room only. Sit in the worst seat yourself, in the daylight, with the lights as they will be on Sunday. Fixing what a single honest tester finds is worth more than another hour spent on the design.

Finally, keep the settings that worked. Save an accessible theme as your default, so that a new volunteer does not have to remember all the rules to get a readable slide.

Putting it into practice

You do not need to redesign everything at once. Pick the theme you use most, raise the contrast, check the size from the back row and slow down or remove the motion.
