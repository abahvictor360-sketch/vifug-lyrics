A visitor joins your livestream from another city. The man on screen is speaking well and has been for ten minutes, and they have no idea who he is. Is he the senior pastor? A guest? A missionary? There is nothing on screen to say. A small bar with a name and a title under it would have answered that in a second.

That small bar is a lower third. Used well, it is nearly invisible and very helpful. Used badly, it covers a face, hangs around for a minute, or shows up in a font nobody can read. This post explains what lower thirds are, when to use them, a handful of design rules, how long they should stay up, and how Vifug handles both speaker name bars and lyric lower thirds.

What a lower third is

The name comes from television: a graphic in the lower third of the screen, usually over a camera picture. In a church setting there are two common kinds.

  • A speaker name bar. A name, and often a title or role under it: "Pastor Daniel Okafor" and "Guest speaker". It tells viewers who is talking.
  • A lyrics or Scripture lower third. The words sit in a band across the bottom of the camera picture instead of covering all of it, so online viewers still see the room.

The room usually does not need either. The people there know who is preaching, and they have full-screen words on the projector. Lower thirds are mostly for people watching on a screen: the livestream, a recording, or an overflow room with a camera feed.

When to use them, and when to skip them

Use a name bar when:

  • a speaker appears for the first time in the service, or the first time in a while;
  • a guest, visiting minister or testimony giver is on camera;
  • someone is introduced and viewers have no other way to learn the name;
  • you are streaming to people who do not know your team.

Skip it when the person has already been named on screen in the last few minutes, when the shot is too tight for a bar to fit without covering the face, or when you are in the middle of prayer or a quiet moment where anything appearing would feel like an interruption. A name bar is a courtesy, not a constant.

For lyrics over camera, a lower third helps online viewers who would otherwise lose the picture of the room behind a wall of text. It is less useful in a building where the words are already on the projector.

Design rules that save you trouble

  1. Keep it low and small. It should sit in the bottom fifth of the picture, to one side, and not reach across the whole frame. Leave the speaker's face and hands free.
  2. Respect the safe margin. Keep the bar away from the very edge. Streaming platforms and TVs crop and overlay their own graphics, and a bar flush to the bottom may sit behind a player control.
  3. One or two lines only. A name, then a short title. A full biography does not belong on screen.
  4. Strong contrast, always. The camera picture changes constantly, so the bar needs a solid or semi-solid backing in a colour that works over any shot. Text straight over video relies on an outline and shadow, and a bar is more reliable.
  5. Use your church's colours, once. One accent colour that matches your logo is enough. A rainbow of effects looks like a different service.
  6. Keep the style the same every week. People should recognise it without reading it.
  7. Round the corners or keep them square, but choose one. Mixing the two across graphics looks careless.

The type rules are the same as for lyrics: plain, bold, large enough to read on a phone. Remember that most livestream viewers are watching on a small screen, so the name has to be legible at the size of a thumbnail. If you can read it on your own phone at arm's length, it is probably fine.

Timing: how long, how often

A bar that appears too briefly cannot be read, and one that lingers becomes clutter. Useful rules of thumb:

  • Bring it up a few seconds after the person starts speaking, not on the first word.
  • Leave it on for roughly 5 to 8 seconds for a name and title. A short name needs less, a long title needs more.
  • Take it away before a camera change or a scripture reading, never during.
  • Show it once per appearance, then trust viewers to remember. Repeating it every two minutes feels like a watermark.

The person on the desk should know the cue in advance. Agree it before the service: "Show his name when he finishes the greeting." Fumbling for a button after a good line is how you end up with a name appearing as the speaker walks off.

Speaker name bars in Vifug

Vifug treats a speaker name bar as a theme you design, then puts it over a camera. Here is the path the guide describes.

  1. Make the theme. Open Settings, then Theme designer, press New, and set For to Speaker name (lower third). A new theme starts as a name bar at the bottom left, on a blue bar, sliding in from the left. The canvas switches to a Speaker sample, a name with the title under it in smaller type.
  2. Design it. The text box is the bar, so move and size it. Under Colours, Bar behind the words turns the bar on, with its colour and rounded corners. Pick the font, text colour and motion, and add your logo as a picture layer if you want one on the bar. Auto-fit makes the name as large as the box allows, so make the box the size you want the bar to be.
  3. Put it on air. Go to the Media tab, then Capture, choose your camera, and under Preacher nameplate type the name and title. In My design, choose your theme. The name bar appears over the camera in your design.

Speaker name themes are kept out of the lyric and Bible theme lists, so they will not get mixed up with your song themes by accident.

If you do not need a custom design, the Preacher nameplate has built-in styles: Classic, Broadcast (a slanted bar with the title beneath), Social (name plus an @handle bar), Pill (a rounded bar with a logo or photo badge) and Minimal (text only), each with an accent colour you can change. To hide the bar, clear the name field; there is no separate on/off switch, an empty name simply does not show. The nameplate shows on every capture layout, full screen, lower third or side by side, and it is independent of any lyrics band, so you can show a guest's name while Scripture runs along the bottom.

As with all Vifug output, the Preview to Live habit applies: you can check how the camera picture and the nameplate look in Preview before anything reaches the screen.

Lyrics as a lower third for online viewers

For lyrics, Vifug gives you two routes.

A theme for the stream. In the theme designer, use the Lower third recipe from the guide: New theme, the Lower third preset, Bottom, untick Auto-fit and set a size around 56, Left align, Outline 2. Then set the Stream overlay row to this theme under Settings, Lyrics, Theme for each screen. The room keeps full-screen words while viewers get a bar across the bottom of the camera picture. A fixed size is the sensible choice here, because the size must match from slide to slide on a stream.

Lyrics over a live camera. Choose Lyrics over video in the capture picker, and a settings panel appears with Full screen or Lower third, Band height and Band width sliders, and a 3 by 3 grid for where the words sit inside the band. You can give the band a colour or an image of your own, or choose No background, where the words sit directly on the video with a heavy shadow and outline.

The stream overlay is the same one you would use in OBS or vMix as a transparent browser source, so a lower third designed in Vifug can sit over your camera in whatever software you use to mix the broadcast. The guide's overlay chapter covers that setup.

A short checklist

  • Is the bar low, small and clear of the face?
  • Can you read it on a phone?
  • Is the name spelled correctly, with the title the person actually uses?
  • Does the desk know when to bring it up and take it down?
  • Have you checked it over the actual camera shot in Preview?
  • Is the Stream overlay row pointing at the right theme?

Ask the speaker how they want their name written. It takes ten seconds and avoids a typo in front of everyone watching.
