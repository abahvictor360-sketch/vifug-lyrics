Someone on the team finds a gorgeous looping video of waves, drops it behind every song, and for a week everyone loves it. Then the laptop fan starts howling, a bright foam crest passes right behind the word "holy", and a volunteer asks where the file came from. Nobody remembers.

Backgrounds are the part of slide design that feels like the fun part, and it is also where most of the trouble starts: legibility, licensing and performance. This post is about making those three choices on purpose. It covers when a still is better than motion, how to think about licences without becoming a lawyer, what keeps files and playback light, and how to set the whole thing up in Vifug.

Still or motion: decide by the job

Neither is better. They do different jobs.

Still imageLooping video
LegibilityPredictable; you can check the worst spot onceChanges every frame; the worst spot keeps moving
Computer loadVery lightHeavier, especially on older machines or with several screens
MoodCalm, steady, traditionalEnergetic, contemporary, immersive
DistractionLowCan pull the eye away from the words
File sizeUsually smallCan be large

A reasonable pattern is stills for slow songs, readings and prayer, and a slow, soft loop for upbeat songs where energy helps. Many teams use one loop for a whole set and keep everything else still. If the motion is the first thing a visitor remembers about the service, it is probably too strong.

What makes a motion background work

  • Slow movement. Drifting clouds, light through water, gentle particles. Fast cuts, camera shake and sudden flashes pull attention and are tiring over a 20-minute set.
  • A seamless loop. If the clip visibly jumps when it restarts, people see it every ten seconds. Preview the join, not just the beginning.
  • Low contrast inside the picture. A calm, mid-dark image has no bright spots for words to get lost in. High-contrast scenes with a sun or a strong highlight are the ones that fail.
  • No sound. A loop behind lyrics should be silent. Otherwise it fights the band.
  • No faces, logos or text. A recognisable face looping behind a song is distracting, and baked-in text collides with your lyrics.

Brightness and legibility

This is where backgrounds succeed or fail. The words have to be readable on the brightest frame, not the average one. Light pictures swallow white text. Three fixes, in order of preference:

  1. Choose a darker picture to start with. Much easier than rescuing a bright one.
  2. Dim it. Vifug's theme designer has a Dim the background picture control, from 0 to 90 percent, which darkens a picture or video behind the words. Its guide says 30 to 50 percent suits most photographs.
  3. Add an outline, a shadow or a bar behind the words. The designer has all three: Outline (0 to 8 pixels), Shadow, and a Bar behind the words with its own colour and corner rounding.

Test a video background by stepping through a long song in Preview and watching the loop for at least a full cycle. Pause on the brightest moment and look at the words on top of it. If that frame is readable, every other frame will be.

Licensing, in plain terms

I am not a lawyer and this is not legal advice, but a few habits keep most teams out of trouble.

  • Finding it online does not mean you may use it. A picture or clip being easy to download says nothing about the licence.
  • Read the licence for the use you have in mind. Showing on screen in a service and streaming it online can be treated differently. Some licences cover personal use only, and some forbid use in a broadcast.
  • Prefer sources that state the terms clearly. Paid libraries, subscription motion-background sites, and sources that publish a plain licence are easier than guessing.
  • Keep a record. A simple spreadsheet with the file name, where it came from, the licence and the date is enough. In two years, you will want it.
  • Your own photos and video are the safest. Shoot a sunrise, a ceiling, a stained window, or a slow pan across the hall. Make sure there are no recognisable people unless you have their permission.

The same caution applies to anything you stream. A background that is fine in the room might need different permission online, so check if your service goes out on the internet. When in doubt, ask the person who licensed it.

File size and performance

A still picture costs the computer almost nothing. A video is decoded in real time, once for every screen showing it, and that adds up when you are also driving a projector, a stage display and a stream. Some guidance that holds on most computers:

  • Match the picture to the screen. A 1920 by 1080 image fills an HD projector; a 6000-pixel photo straight from a camera is wasted and slower to load. Resize first.
  • Keep loops short and compressed. A 10 to 30 second loop at 1080p is plenty. Longer clips and higher resolutions add size without any gain you can see on a wall.
  • Use a common format. MP4 with H.264 plays almost everywhere. Check how it plays on the actual computer that runs your service.
  • Test with everything running. Open the projector, the stage display and the stream, then play the loop for five minutes. Dropped frames or a loud fan are your answer.
  • Keep a plain fallback. A dark still of the same colour palette costs nothing and saves the day if a video misbehaves.

Backgrounds in Vifug

There are three places to set a background in Vifug, and which you use depends on what you want to change.

  • Media tab. Add a picture or video, click it, and in the properties panel use "Use as background for" and choose Lyrics, Bible or Presentations. Press the same button again to take it back off. For a video, check Playback first: set it to Silent and Loop for a background loop.
  • Theme designer. The Background picture or video section lets you pick one from your library, upload a file, paste a link or choose a plain colour. It is saved with the theme and travels with it wherever that theme is used.
  • Settings. Lyrics, Bible and Presentations each have a Background section, if you would rather pick it while setting up.

Scripture can have a quieter background than songs. Setting one for the Bible in the Media tab switches Scripture off sharing the lyric background. A slide with its own background wins over the default, and so does a background set on the deck itself. A single song can also carry its own look: open the song in the editor, and the "This song's own look" panel lets you set a theme, a background and a text colour for just that song, each one optional.

The background you choose flows through to the projector, the stream overlay and the stage display automatically, so you are not setting it three times. If a background does not change on a screen, the guide's first suggestion is to check whether Bible or Presentations has one of its own.

A small checklist before Sunday

  1. Is the licence recorded, and does it cover streaming if you stream?
  2. Is the loop silent and does it join cleanly?
  3. Is the brightest frame readable with the words on it?
  4. Is the file a sensible size for the screen?
  5. Did you run it with the projector, stage display and stream all on?
  6. Do you have a plain fallback ready?
