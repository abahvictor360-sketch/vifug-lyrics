You run the screens at a school assembly, a conference, a community concert or a studio show, and every tutorial you find assumes a church. The examples are about hymns and Scripture, and you just need to put a speaker's name on a camera shot and a countdown on a wall. Is software built for worship any use to you?

Often it is, because the underlying job is the same. Someone at a desk controls what a room and sometimes a stream see, while the content changes as the event unfolds. This post walks through six settings outside church, what each needs, and how a live tool such as Vifug can serve them. It also says where a different tool is the better pick.

What carries over from church to everywhere else

Strip out the hymns and a church service is a live event with a running order, a stage, a room, and often an online audience. The features that matter transfer directly:

  • A private preview before anything goes out. Useful anywhere a mistake is public.
  • Text from a library, not hand-built slides. Lyrics, quotes, poems, sponsor lists and speaker names are all just text that needs to look consistent.
  • Several screens from one machine. Main display, stage monitor, overflow room.
  • Cameras, lower thirds and timers. The tools of any live show.
  • Output to a stream or a vision mixer. Overlays for OBS and vMix, or NDI.

If you are still working out whether you want a slide tool or a live tool at all, start with what presentation software is.

Choosing the right mode in Vifug

When you first launch Vifug, it asks what the app is for: Church, Media and entertainment, or Presentations only. This matters outside church.

  • Media and entertainment is for shows, events, conferences, karaoke and live streams. The Bible and the hymn library are left out, the Plans tab is called Run of show, and you start with a small set of public-domain songs to try.
  • Presentations only is just slides, pictures and video to your screens.
  • Church keeps everything, including the Bible and service plans.

Nothing is deleted by the choice, and you can change it under Settings, General, then What Vifug is for.

Schools and assemblies

An assembly mixes a song or two, notices, a slideshow of the week, a video from a class, and perhaps a live camera. The order moves when a teacher runs long, and the person at the desk is often a staff member or a student helper with limited training.

What helps

  • Preview before live, so a helper cannot put the wrong class photo up in front of the whole school.
  • An announcement ticker. In Vifug it is a line of text that scrolls along the bottom and keeps going even when the screen is blank, which suits a bus-delay notice or a room change. It shows on the projector and the stream overlay, and you switch it off when it has done its job.
  • A countdown timer to the start of the assembly, and a count-up for how long a speaker has taken.
  • A phone or tablet remote, so a teacher can advance slides from the front.

For the slide content itself, teachers often arrive with PowerPoint decks. Save them as PDF and import them to keep the design.

Conferences and talks

A conference runs several speakers in a row, each with their own deck. The operator's problems are handovers, names on screen, timing and a stage that needs a different view from the audience.

What helps

  • Lower thirds for speaker names. In the theme designer you can make a theme for Speaker name (lower third), with a bar, a font, a logo and a slide-in motion. The nameplate over a camera feed also offers several styles (Classic, Broadcast, Social, Pill and Minimal), and you can show a name without any lyrics band at all.
  • A stage display for the speaker: the current slide, the next slide and a clock.
  • A run of show listing each session in order, so the operator can cue the next one directly.
  • A countdown that can be shown on the stage screen but not the main one, since the audience does not need to watch the clock.

Speakers bring different formats. A PDF imports exactly as designed, but its text is not editable inside the app, so a change goes back to the original file and gets re-imported.

Studios and online shows

A studio show cares more about the output going to a mixer than about a projector. The operator layers graphics over a camera shot and sends the result to viewers.

What helps

  • A transparent overlay as a Browser source in OBS or vMix. The Teleport feature, in Settings, Streaming and output, adds it to OBS in one click, provided OBS 28 or newer has its WebSocket server enabled. For vMix it adds a Web Browser input.
  • Native NDI output from the desktop app, so another machine on the network can take the graphics without a capture card.
  • Different pictures for different audiences. The guide describes full-screen words in the room and a lower third over the camera for viewers online, set under Streaming and output.
  • Cameras and capture cards brought in directly, with layouts for full screen, words over video, or side by side, plus a chroma key if you shoot against a green backdrop.

A caution: live streaming, recording and NDI need the desktop app, not the browser version. The streaming chapter covers canvas size, encoder choice and a private test run.

Concerts and live events

For a concert or community performance, the screen might carry song lyrics for a singalong, sponsor logos, a countdown to the start, and visuals behind the artist.

What helps

  • Looping video backgrounds behind lyrics, and the option to mute their sound.
  • A logo in a corner. In Settings, General, then Logo, you can place a transparent PNG in the top left or right, choose its size and opacity, and keep it away from the timer.
  • Blank on a key. Space cuts the screen to black without losing your place, handy between acts.
  • Remappable shortcuts, so a presenter clicker or keyboard layout you already know works. Settings, then Shortcuts lets you rebind them.

Sound matters at events. The guide explains that a laptop connected by HDMI often keeps sending audio to its own speakers, so you choose the PA under Settings, General, then Sound output and press Test before the doors open.

Venues and community halls

A hall hosts different groups: a yoga class on Tuesday, a council meeting on Thursday, a wedding at the weekend. Each wants something different on the wall, and the person managing the venue is rarely a technician.

Here, the value is in a short learning path and a predictable screen. A new hire can learn cue, check, send in a few minutes. Extra screens, such as a foyer display, can be added by name under Streaming and output, then Extra screens, and opened on a monitor or on any device with a browser. For a hall whose Wi-Fi is unreliable, the desktop app is the safer base because running it needs no internet.

Signage and unattended screens

Here we should be honest. A live tool is built for someone at the controls. For a lobby display that should run itself for months, with scheduling and fleet management, a dedicated signage product is likely a better match, and you should look at what those offer.

A live tool still has a role at the edges. It can run the welcome loop in the half hour before an event, then switch to live content when the doors open. If that fits, a looping video or slide set with the timer running is a workable pre-show.

A short checklist for a non-church event

  1. Choose the mode that matches (Media and entertainment, or Presentations only).
  2. Import the decks you have, saving designed ones as PDF first.
  3. Build a lower third for names and test it over the actual camera.
  4. Decide which screens carry the timer and which do not.
  5. Set the sound output and test it in the room.
  6. Rehearse the cue, check, send rhythm with the real operator.
  7. Run a private test stream if you broadcast.
