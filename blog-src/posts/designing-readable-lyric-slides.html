You sit in the back row during the first song and the words are there, technically. Thin letters on a photo of a sunrise, bright sky behind white text, the last line sitting so close to the bottom edge that the projector cuts it off. Everyone up front can sing from memory, so nobody complains. The visitors just stand and move their lips.

Readable lyrics are not a talent. They come from a handful of decisions you make once, in a theme, and then stop thinking about. This post walks through those decisions in the order that matters: how far people sit, what the letters look like, how much contrast you have, what to put behind the words, and how much edge to leave. Along the way I will show where each one lives in Vifug's theme designer, since that is the tool I will use as the worked example.

Start with the distance, not the font

A slide is read from the farthest seat that matters, not from the sound desk. Walk to the back row on a Saturday with the projector on and a test slide up. If you cannot read the second line without leaning forward, nobody in the back can either, no matter how nice the font looks on your laptop.

A rough working rule that many media teams use: the height of a capital letter on the wall should be at least 1/200 of the distance to the farthest viewer. At 20 metres, that is around 10 cm. You do not need to measure it every week. Measure once, note the result, and use it to judge whether a size feels right on a screen you cannot walk up to.

Words in a worship slide are also read in a hurry. People are singing, not studying. That pushes you toward larger type, fewer words, and plainer letter shapes than you would use on a handout.

Choose a typeface that survives a projector

Projectors blur edges slightly and low-end ones lose fine detail. A few traits make a typeface hold up:

  • Open, simple letters. A sans-serif with a clear difference between a lowercase l, a capital I and the number 1 is easier to read than a decorative one.
  • Even stroke thickness. Fonts with hairline strokes next to heavy ones (some serifs, many script faces) lose their thin parts at distance.
  • Generous spacing. Letters that sit too tight turn into smudges when blurred.
  • A real bold. Choose a font that has genuine weights rather than a regular one pushed thicker by software.

Script and handwriting fonts are fine for a title card, a word like "Welcome" that is on screen for ten seconds. For a verse of a song that people read in real time, they cost you readers.

In Vifug, the Font control in the theme designer lists three groups: your own fonts, the app's fonts, and the fonts installed on the computer. You can add a .ttf, .otf, .woff or .woff2 file (up to about 6 MB), and it is kept with your library, so it is included in backups and works on every screen, phones and tablets used as screens included. A system font only works on computers that have it installed, so if the projector runs on a different machine from the one where you designed the theme, add the file instead. Only add fonts you have the right to use.

Weight and size

Weight matters more than most people expect. Light and thin weights look elegant on a monitor and disappear on a wall. Vifug's designer has a weight setting from 300 (light) to 900 (black), and 600 to 700 reads well from the back of a room. Start there.

For size, you have two choices in the designer:

  1. Auto-fit on. The words grow or shrink to fill the text box, so two lines come out large and six lines smaller. This is the right choice for most lyric themes, because a short slide gets big type for free.
  2. Auto-fit off with a fixed size. Every slide uses the same size no matter how long it is. Use this when consistency matters more than filling the box, for example a lower third on a stream.

If long slides overflow the box with a fixed size, make the box bigger, lower the size, or show fewer lines per slide (see how many lines to put on each slide).

Contrast first, colour second

White text on a dark background and pale yellow on dark blue are the classics because they work. The real test is not the colour pair but the worst frame: the brightest part of the picture sitting right behind a word.

  • Keep the text and the area behind it far apart in lightness. Squint at the preview: if the words blur into the background when you squint, the contrast is too low.
  • Avoid red on green, or any pair that looks the same in greyscale. Some people in your room are colour-blind, and projectors wash colours out anyway.
  • Pure white on pure black is harsh in a dark room, but it is far better than something pretty that nobody can read. Slightly off-white is a reasonable middle.
  • Be careful with coloured text over photos. A saturated colour in the middle of a busy picture is the first thing that fails.

The designer has a Text colour and a Background colour. For a plain background, set the two and check them on the samples.

Help the words with a dim, an outline, a shadow or a bar

When the background is a photograph or a video, contrast stops being a single choice and becomes a fight on every frame. You have four tools, from gentle to strong, and the designer has all of them:

ToolWhat it doesWhere in Vifug's designer
DimDarkens the whole picture or video behind the wordsColours: Dim the background picture, 0 to 90 percent
OutlineA line around each letterOutline and Outline colour, 0 to 8 pixels
ShadowA soft drop shadow behind the wordsShadow slider; all the way left turns it off
Bar behind the wordsA solid band behind the text, with its own colour and rounded cornersColours: Bar behind the words

Vifug's own guidance is that 30 to 50 percent dimming suits most photographs, and that a 2 pixel black outline keeps white words readable over a busy picture. Those are sensible starting numbers. Begin with the dim, because it improves the whole slide and costs nothing in style. Add an outline or a shadow if the picture still has bright spots. Reach for the bar when the background is too busy to tame any other way, or when the words are over a live camera shot.

One caution: an outline that is too thick makes letters look clumsy, and a heavy shadow turns into a grey fog on a light picture. Small amounts of each, together, usually beat a lot of one.

Safe margins and the text box

Every room is cropped differently. A projector can overshoot the wall at the top, a TV may lose an edge to overscan, and the screen's bottom corner may sit behind a speaker stack. Words close to the edge are the first to be cut.

Vifug has two things to set here. First, the text box in the theme designer: the dashed green box on the slide is where the words sit. You can drag it, resize it, use presets such as Full screen, Lower third or Centre card, or type exact values. A Left of 6 and a Width of 88 leaves 6 percent clear on each side. Second, Settings, Lyrics, Text margin: this is the screen's own safe margin, and it applies on top of the box, so words are never pushed off a projector that crops its edges. You can use one number for all four sides, or set each side when only one is being cut.

To find out what your room does, put up a slide with words on every edge and look at the wall. Raise the margin on the edge that is cut and leave the others alone. Vifug's guide notes that type which sizes itself to the slide gets bigger as you bring the margin down, so there is a trade: a wide margin is safe but small, a tight margin is large but risky. Fix the problem edge, not all four.

Check it with real content before Sunday

A theme that looks great on two short lines can fall apart on a long verse or a Scripture reading. The designer has samples under the slide: 2 lines, 4 lines, Scripture, Speaker and Slide. Click through all of them before you trust a theme. Then do a real check:

  1. Open a long song and step through the slides in Preview, not on the live screen.
  2. Look at the slide with the brightest part of your background behind the text.
  3. Stand at the back of the room, or as far from the screen as you can.
  4. Look at the corners and edges for any cut-off words.
  5. Ask someone who has not seen the slide before to read a line aloud.

One more habit: design on a copy. Changes in the theme designer go to the live screen immediately for any screen using that theme, so during a service make a duplicate, work on that, and switch to it when it is ready.
